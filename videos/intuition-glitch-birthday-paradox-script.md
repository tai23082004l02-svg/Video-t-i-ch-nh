# Only 23 People? The Birthday Paradox

How many strangers would you need in a room before a shared birthday becomes more likely than not?

A hundred? Two hundred?

Try twenty-three.

If that feels wrong, hold on to that feeling.

It points to the exact mistake this puzzle exposes.

You may be imagining someone matching your birthday.

But nobody has to match you.

Any two people can match.

And every person who enters creates more chances than the one before.

The second person creates one possible pair.

The third adds two more.

By the time twenty-three people are standing here, there are two hundred and fifty-three pairs.

You were looking at a small crowd. The probability was looking at a much bigger network.

But counting pairs is only the clue. Here is the calculation.

Instead of asking when birthdays collide,

ask what it takes for every birthday to be different.

The first person can have any birthday.

The second must avoid one occupied date.

That leaves three hundred and sixty-four out of three hundred and sixty-five days.

If both birthdays are different, the third must avoid two dates.

Now three hundred and sixty-three days remain.

Keep multiplying those chances as people enter.

At twenty-three people, the chance that every birthday is different is about forty-nine point three percent.

So at least one shared birthday is the rest:

about fifty point seven percent.

For this classic calculation, assume independent birthdays,

equally likely across three hundred and sixty-five days. Ignore leap day.

That does not promise a match in this particular room.

It means that across many such rooms, just over half would have one.

And with fifty people, the chance exceeds ninety-seven percent.

The surprise was never how many birthdays fit in a year.

It was how many comparisons fit in a room.

Your intuition counted people. The probability counted pairs.

## Mathematical model

Independent birthdays, equally likely on 365 days; leap day ignored. P(shared) = 1 − ∏(365−k)/365 for k=0..n−1. At n=23: 0.5072972343239857. At n=50: 0.9703735795779884. Pair count does not make matching-pair events independent.
